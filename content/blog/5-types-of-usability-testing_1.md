As a founder or C suite leader, you're constantly balancing growth, product market fit, and operational efficiency. While strategy and vision often dominate boardroom conversations, one of the most impactful (yet underestimated) levers for business success is **usability testing**.

Why? Because **every friction point in your product is a cost**, whether in lost conversions, higher support tickets, or declining retention. Usability testing doesn't just improve design; it safeguards revenue. Here are five types of usability testing that leaders should know, complete with their business value, dos and don'ts, and practical leadership takeaways.

## 1. Moderated Usability Testing

In moderated testing, a facilitator (often a UX researcher) sits with the user, either in person or virtually, and asks them to perform specific tasks. While the user works, the facilitator observes their actions, listens to their thought process, and asks clarifying questions like "What made you click there?" or "What do you expect will happen next?".

**Business Value for Leaders:**

- Provides *rich qualitative insights* that reveal why customers behave a certain way.
- Helps validate assumptions before investing in development, reducing wasted spend.
- Ideal for **high stakes launches** (new pricing models, onboarding flows, checkout redesigns).

**Do's:**

- Use open ended questions like *"What are you thinking as you try this?"*
- Recruit users who closely resemble your ideal customers.
- Record sessions for internal alignment with product and leadership teams through tools like [Maze](https://maze.co/).

**Don'ts:**

- Don't "teach" users how to use the product during testing.
- Don't recruit only internal employees; they're biased.
- Don't treat small sample sizes as statistically representative; use them to uncover patterns, not numbers.

👉 **Founder takeaway:** Moderated testing is your **boardroom insurance policy**. It reduces the risk of million dollar missteps by catching user pain points early.

## 2. Unmoderated Remote Testing

In unmoderated testing, users are **given a set of tasks to complete on their own, without a facilitator present.** The sessions are usually recorded, and you later review where they struggled or succeeded. For example, you might ask: *"Sign up for a free trial and cancel your subscription."* You then see if they succeed, how long it takes, and where they stumble.

**Business Value for Leaders:**

- Low cost and scalable, test with dozens of users without heavy overhead.
- Reveals how customers interact with your product *in their real world environment*.
- Perfect for **iterative improvements** where speed and volume matter (such as testing multiple UI variants quickly).

**Do's:**

- Keep tasks simple and time bound (5 to 10 minutes max).
- Use analytics tools to automatically capture completion rates and error points, like [UserTesting](https://www.usertesting.com/).
- Run frequent, small tests to keep a pulse on customer experience.

**Don'ts:**

- Don't use unmoderated testing for very complex workflows; it may create confusion.
- Don't forget demographic diversity if you're scaling internationally.
- Don't rely solely on text feedback; pair it with session recordings.

👉 **Founder takeaway:** Think of this as your **fastest route to market validation**. It lets your teams ship with confidence, without slowing velocity.

## 3. A/B Testing

A/B testing is a **controlled experiment where you show two (or more) variations of a screen, page, or feature to different sets of users.** You then measure which version performs better on a defined metric (conversion rate, click throughs, trial signups).

For example, you might show half your visitors a green CTA button and half a red one, then measure which leads to more purchases.

**Business Value for Leaders:**

- Provides hard, quantitative evidence for decisions.
- Directly ties design changes to financial outcomes: CAC, LTV, conversion rates.
- Allows you to iterate toward **incremental revenue gains** without risking large redesign failures through tools like [AB Smartly](https://absmartly.com/).

**Do's:**

- Test one variable at a time (button placement, headline, pricing).
- Define success metrics *before* the test (CTR, MRR impact, etc.).
- Run tests long enough to reach statistical significance.

**Don'ts:**

- Don't "cherry pick" short term results; early spikes may flatten out.
- Don't A/B test without a hypothesis; testing everything wastes time.
- Don't ignore the cost of engineering resources for small tests.

👉 **Founder takeaway:** A/B testing speaks your language, **data, and ROI**. It connects UX improvements directly to business metrics you report to investors.

## 4. Card Sorting & Tree Testing

These methods focus on **how users organize and navigate information** in your product.

- **Card Sorting:** Users are given "cards" (features, categories, pages) and asked to group and label them. This shows how they expect your content to be structured.
- **Tree Testing:** Users are asked to find specific items in a simplified menu structure (like a bare bones sitemap). This reveals whether your navigation is intuitive.

For example, in a SaaS product, you might test whether users expect 'Billing' under 'Account Settings' or under 'Subscription.'

**Business Value for Leaders:**

- Reduces drop offs by making products intuitive and easy to navigate.
- Prevents expensive reworks of complex navigation systems.
- Increases adoption in **multi feature products** like SaaS platforms, marketplaces, or eCommerce.

**Do's:**

- Use both open (users create categories) and closed (users fit content into categories) card sorts.
- Prioritize clarity over creativity in labeling, simple wins.
- Pair card sorting insights with analytics (where users drop off).

**Don'ts:**

- Don't assume internal jargon is user friendly.
- Don't rely on one test; different segments may organize differently.
- Don't overcomplicate; users expect efficiency, not novelty, in navigation.

👉 **Founder takeaway:** If users can't find what they need, they won't buy. Card sorting and tree testing are **direct defenses against churn and lost revenue.**

## 5. Eye Tracking & Heatmaps

Eye tracking uses specialized software (or hardware like webcams) to track **where users look on a screen, in what order, and for how long.** Heatmaps visualize this by showing areas of high attention (red "hot spots") vs. ignored areas (blue/cold spots).

For example, you might learn that users' eyes are drawn to a banner ad instead of the "Sign Up" button, explaining low conversions.

**Business Value for Leaders:**

- Reveals subconscious user behavior, what captures (or loses) attention.
- Optimizes high value screens (landing pages, checkout, pricing tables).
- Increases ROI on marketing spend by ensuring key CTAs actually get noticed.

**Do's:**

- Use heatmaps alongside scroll maps to understand full page engagement.
- Prioritize testing on revenue critical pages.
- Combine findings with A/B testing for the best results.

**Don'ts:**

- Don't assume "more attention = better" (sometimes distraction is harmful).
- Don't test low impact pages; focus on conversion critical flows.
- Don't over interpret one session; look for consistent patterns.

👉 **Founder takeaway:** Eye tracking and heatmaps let you **monetize attention**. Every overlooked CTA is lost revenue; this testing makes sure no pixel is wasted.

## Final Word: Why Leaders Can't Ignore This

Usability testing is not a "design nice to have." It's a **business survival tool**. Whether you're bootstrapped or scaling, the cost of poor usability shows up in three places executives care about most:

- **Revenue leakage:** Customers drop off at checkout or onboarding.
- **Operational costs:** More support tickets and churn handling.
- **Brand equity:** Frustrated users won't return, and they'll tell others.

Investing in usability testing means protecting your roadmap from risk, defending budget allocation with data, and ensuring your product delivers not just functionality, but profitability.
